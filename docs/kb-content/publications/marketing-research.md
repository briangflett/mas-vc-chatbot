# Marketing Research for MAS Clients

Source: https://www.masadvise.org/mas-publications/

Application of Marketing Research for Non-Profit Organizations

Tim Hodapp

Senior staff at non-profit organizations are challenged on a regular basis to make critical, cost-effective decisions.  Marketing research – the process of systematically gathering and analyzing decision-critical information about citizen/service user wants, needs, beliefs and behaviour – can assist in this effort.  While conducting marketing research activities may require some cost to the organization, this can be a worthwhile investment if it leads to more effective, fact-based decisions.

Marketing research is applied under two broad categories of work.  Strategic applications impact the overall direction of an organization.  Tactical research consists of planning, developing and implementing the actual activities for delivering your services to the market. Strategic research supports the idea. Tactical research supports the action.

Specific applications of marketing research for non-profits and typical questions they can answer include, but are not limited to:

Strategic

Organizational/service awareness, usage and attitudes:  What are the levels of awareness and use of the organization and its specific services among the general population or specific target groups?  What opinions do people have of the organization and its services?  Have recent communications and other initiatives had an impact on awareness, usage and opinions?  

Market/user segmentation: What are the demographic, psychographic, motivational, cultural and behavioural characteristics of potential/actual service users?  What communications strategy would resonate best with key target groups?

User decision-making:  What is the relative importance of the various factors that motivate people to use a program or service?  What is the process used to decide on whether to use a program/service or not?

Tactical

Communications:  How well are specific communications initiatives meeting objectives for building awareness, image and use?  Is the format working effectively?  Is the content too complex or not detailed enough?

Program/service:  To what extent is your program/service meeting user needs and expectations?  Where can improvements be made?  

Pricing: If a charge is applied for program/service use, what features are most valuable to potential users and how much are they willing to pay for them.  What is the most basic service that can be offered for the lowest price?  What other aspects can be offered as optional add-ons?

User satisfaction/experience/loyalty:  Are program/service users satisfied with the overall experience as well as with each of the touchpoints included in using a given program/service? Are users likely to continue with the program/service or will they look for another provider or stop altogether? 

Mystery usage:  Is the program or service being delivered as intended?  Is training needed to ensure compliance by service providers with stated standards.

Two main sources of data are generally used for marketing research. Primary research is customized to meet specific information needs with targeted data collected to address the problem/question at hand. Secondary research sources publicly accessible data that has been collected for other purposes. Secondary research generally costs far less than primary research, but seldom comes in a form that exactly meets the needs of the researcher.

Two main methods are used for primary research.  Qualitative research is generally used for exploratory purposes, producing results not generalizable to the whole population.  Examples include focus groups, in-depth interviews and projective techniques.  Quantitative research allows for conclusions to be made with a given level of confidence from results gained through systematic surveys among relatively large samples of a target population.  

Certainly, marketing research has its limitations.  It is not an exact science, even though it uses the techniques of science.  The results of any research project are only as good as the people who manage the collection, analysis and reporting of data and the staff who apply the findings and insights to their organization.

As you may know, MAS’ mandate is limited to advising clients on problems or issues of importance.  The client is responsible for implementing or operationalizing the actions resulting from that consulting activity with MAS.  For marketing research activities, a MAS volunteer consultant would serve as the in-house research manager for the client.  We would, in consultation with the client:

Develop the objectives and scope of the project

Design the project specifications and methodology

Prepare a Request for Information (RFI) or Request for Proposal (RFP) to be sent to potential vendors

Assist in the review of RFI/RFP responses and the selection of vendors as needed

Review any questionnaires (for quantitative research) or discussion guides (for qualitative research) prepared by the selected vendor

Monitor the work of the vendor in terms of sample selection, data collection, data analysis, report/presentation preparation

Facilitate action-planning workshops based on project results.

Aspects of a research project that may incur costs for the client include:

Purchase of names of potential study respondents (sample)

Recruitment and incentive fees for respondents

Rental of facilities, if needed, for qualitative sessions or for quantitative in-person interviews.

Contracting with a third-party researcher for such activities as questionnaire development, data collection, data analysis, report/presentation preparation.
