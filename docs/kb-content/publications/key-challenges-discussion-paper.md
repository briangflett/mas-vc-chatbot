Pondering some new approaches to four key organization challenges identified by MAS Clients 

 

PART A: Background 

Management Advisory Service (MAS) is a small Toronto based charity that for 30 years has been recruiting highly skilled (mostly retired) executive and management level individuals and linking them up to provide free consulting to charities and not-for-profit organizations that come to us for help.  Over the years we have supported around 2,000 organizations complete strategic plans, learn about and develop their governance strategies, identify better processes in planning, finances, IT and fundraising, learn the basics of marketing and communication and grapple with human resource challenges, including volunteer engagement. 

Periodically we carry out a formal survey of the organizations we've helped in order assess our relevance to them, and to identify the areas where NFPs will need the most help moving forward.  Our 2024 survey results provide an interesting glimpse into the reality of the 255 organizations that sent in responses.  The following paper provides a very brief summary of top line results and a discussion on some of the key challenges identified by respondents.

The MAS Client Survey Respondents 

An online survey was sent September 2024 to 2,886 current and former MAS "clients" (charities and not-for-profits who have received pro bono consulting help over the past 10+ years).  

A total of N=255 completed survey responses were received. Six out of ten respondents were registered charities – the rest incorporated as not-for-profit organizations. 

The majority operate within the GTA and come from the following sub-sectors; education, social services, recreation/culture, healthcare and housing.  The majority identify a focus on support provided to marginalized populations. 

63% operate organizations with fewer than nine employees (53 of them having no paid staff at all).  In terms of financial status, a broad range is represented with 20% of respondents from organizations with annual funding under $50,000, 32% over $1M and the rest in between. 

A quarter of the organizations have been in existence for more than 15 years and 36% under five years. 

In summary, the respondents to the MAS Client survey reflect the usual – and wonderful - diversity of the Canadian charity/NFP sector.  They run the gamut of size from tiny to pretty big, (annual budgets over $3M) many operating with very few paid staff - although 30 of them reported having 50 or more paid employees.  Some are brand new while others have a long history of service.  Virtually all identified providing services to "marginalized" populations including low income, homeless, BIPOC, at-risk youth, seniors and children, people living with mental illness, disability or addictions, with a handful working in advocacy, policy or research. 

What they told us: their topmost challenges and priorities 

As below respondents identified their biggest challenge as funding, with recruiting and retaining volunteers (including Board members) as another. 

When asked to identify where they need consulting help, respondents' top choices were in the areas of fundraising and strategic planning.  

And in response to an invitation to comment on the critical issues, the following themes emerged from the hundred of comments provided: 

Fundraising/financing… There isn't enough, we need to know how to do it better (how to explain or promote our work), how to find Board or volunteers to fundraise.  No growth in government funding while operations are more expensive. Developing a sustainable funding model. Funders directing us and fund applications, processes and reporting very complicated/onerous… 

Volunteers: Loss of volunteers for a variety of reasons (age, post pandemic, interpersonal issues, increase in demand), we need more and "better" volunteers, we have issues keeping volunteers and this is different/more urgent than in the past.  The same applies to Board members – losing them, keeping them.  We need to clarify Board roles.  Challenges shifting from a hands-on, working Board to governance focused.  The difficulty of not achieving goals because of Board dynamics (opinions, hesitations) … 

Staffing: Senior staff retiring after a long time and so very difficult to replace.  Paying a decent wage, managing retirements, wages vs. inflation, managing difficult employees – making difficult decisions (not a skillset we have), managing a lot of different changes.  Having the right technology and the skills to use it… 

Growth: We want/need to grow but can't find the money.  There are more and more demands for our help as people struggle more financially/with addictions/with housing etc. etc.  Our strategic plan identifies growth as a goal. We need more money, more volunteers, better space if we are to grow… 

 

PART B: Discussion 

Issue #1: Funding   

The Challenge 

I don't really need to write very much here, do I?  There is not an organization in the charity and not-for-profit sector that does not have a need for more funding, pointe finale.  

I led a national consultation on voluntary sector issues in 2005 and the top concern of every one of the 1,500 charity organisations who participated, was funding.  I know for certain that nothing has changed in this regard over the past 20 years.  And the urgency around funding doesn't only apply to new or small entities trying to get a toehold in the sector.  Ask any hospital CEO if they need more funding and they will say yes… immediately. 

There are a few matters related to funding beyond the simplest one of there never being enough.  The issue of 'core funding' is relevant to a lot of organizations that live the reality of piecing together monies from funded projects or designated funds, in the absence of it.  The issue of sustainability hovers over many organizations who face uncertainty impacted by things like changes in government policy or downturns in the economy drying up donor funds.  Fundraising tactics are always evolving, as are questions of the capacity of organizations to simultaneously fundraise and deliver on their mission.  But overshadowing all of these is the ever-present concern for virtually every Board of Directors, senior leadership team, and development department; to find the necessary funds to achieve their respective goals. 

A Possible Response 

Pursuing funds will never not be a core activity of not-for-profit organizations and registered charities.  However, there is a common imperative in our sector which tends to focus on continuous growth – and not just growth that aligns with incremental increases related to cost of living, wages etc.  But growth in programs, services, scales of operation, bottom lines.  It is rare that a strategic plan is developed that doesn't identify goals related to 'doing more' or attracting more clients/support/donors/funders.  Operational planning and budgeting are often predicated on such expectations. 

My career as a charity executive was defined by the pressures that come with a desire for – an expectation of - continual growth.  It is only in my current state of semi-retirement and the work we do at MAS helping organizations struggling with the same kinds of stresses, that I have come to question why more, and bigger, are always considered to be better. 

And so, my suggested 'possible response' on matters related to funding, fundraising and growth, is more contrary than affirming.  I would challenge us to consider making the decision to plan operations based on the amount of funds we know we have on hand or can easily raise.  Living - if you will – within our means.   

But Paddy, many will say, demands for our services are growing… there are so many clients out there who need our help… we have to grow to stay relevant etc.  All true, and yet no matter how much larger your organization gets, the fact is there will always be more you could hypothetically do… more people you could serve.  No organization – unless legally mandated to provide universal services (such as hospitals, police services etc.) will ever cover the whole waterfront.  So, why not consider doing what you can, within the resources you have, and focus energy on delivering outstanding services vs. more of them?  Having pride and comfort saying, "this is how much we do and how many we serve"? 

Others have explored similar ideas around 'not growing', either out of pragmatism or in response to external factors.  The following few commentaries are only a scattershot of views on this.  I will leave these other wise commentators to conclude this brief discussion related to funding: 

 

"I feel we are making a huge mistake in aping the relentless desire for year-on-year growth in revenue, often - but not always - for its own sake. Many of us who work in the non-profit sector do so because we believe our organisations should be progressive ones that strive to set a higher bar. Most of the time we do so. However, I can't help but feel that our current obsession with growth is not only unsustainable, it's counter - progressive. We have in too many instances replaced being inspirational with being simply acquisitive. Why? Charities, under increasing pressure to 'run a tight business-like ship', increasingly (but not exclusively) appoint hawkish boards made up of businesspeople with a lifetime of growth-chasing under their belts. And so it goes on". 

"Have we developed an unnecessary obsession with growth in the non-profit sector?"   Toby Bourke, Global Director of Marketing, Communications and Fundraising, Hope and Homes for Children. LinkedIn, May 4, 2017 

 

 

"Survey findings have shown that charities are "scrambling to sustain growth" through diversified revenue strategies amidst economic challenges and increased service demands. However, while this shift demonstrates their resilience and innovation, the rise in business-like strategies could also divert focus from their core missions and strain resources".  

Charities Insights Canada Project, Nguyen, Thi Kim Quy, Postdoctoral Fellow, Carleton University, 2024 

 

"A charity requires clarity about what it is trying to achieve and how it will do that. Awareness of the things you are choosing not to do is as important as understanding what you are doing. This can help give the organisation focus and help it avoid getting distracted. You can't do everything. It is best to concentrate on what you can and do it to the best of your ability. Align the strategy with your charitable mission and resist any temptation to drift away from that". 

"Ten ways to ensure your charity is sustainable", Ian Allsop, Charity Digital.org.UK, January 2025 

 

"Unfortunately, most of the nonprofit sector's funding sources follow economic trends, meaning that when the country is facing economic troubles and demand for the sector's services rise, the sector has less resources than before to meet that demand. Government funding from all levels is one of the few counter-cyclical sources of sector funding, but whether governments will step in to fill funding gaps is an open question, especially at the federal level where there is so much uncertainty".  

What trends will impact charities and nonprofits in the first quarter of 2025? Emily Jensen,  Imagine Canada Blog post, February 5, 2025

Issue #2: Volunteers 

The Challenge 

According to Volunteer Canada more than 65% of all charities and not-for-profit organizations have reported the same challenges recruiting and retaining volunteers as our small sample of agencies has.   

While young people have the highest rate of volunteering, their involvement tends to be sporadic (often one-time, related to education requirements or career goals).  Contrasting this, people over age 55 have a lower rate of volunteering but when they do, they contribute the highest number of hours of any cohort.   

It is no surprise to anyone in our sector that there is hardly an organization that hasn't benefitted from the contribution of a few (or if you're lucky quite a few) "super volunteers" from that group.  People who for years provide countless hours of time, are always ready to help in a variety of ways, whose presence and leadership essentially define the organization.  Unfortunately, these senior volunteers (both in terms of age and experience) are the people organizations are telling us we are losing.  So why have they dropped away?   

The major reason is they have other things to do.  This baby boom generation (currently age 57 to 77) are wedged between aging parents who are living longer than any generation before them, and their adult children facing job, housing, childcare and economic challenges that create greater dependence on their parents.   

As a 68-year-old baby boomer I know from personal experience how true this is.  I, like so many of my peers "left home" when I was 18.  My adult children lived with me into their late twenties.  My mother lived to be 96 and informal caregiving for her was also time consuming.  My personal picture is statistically replicated across my age cohort.   

Baby boomer aged volunteers are also healthier and wealthier than many previous generations.  As a result, they have busy and active lives engaged in travel, hobbies, sport and a myriad of late and post career activities.  Of course this doesn't describe every older volunteer; many don't fall into such an easy generalization. But whatever the reasons, older volunteers, traditionally the backbone of many volunteer programs, have quietly stepped away over the past five or so years, and organizations are feeling the impact.  

A last quick observation, which I'm not sure has been statistically proven yet, but from our survey respondents, and anecdotally in our work at MAS, we are told that when a lot of volunteer work moved into virtual modes during the COVID pandemic, many older people dropped away – and never came back.  Perhaps not liking the shift away from in-person or perhaps finding the time away providing a natural stopping point. 

A Possible Response 

Since my ten years at the helm of Volunteer Canada in the nineties/2000's I have been an advocate for strategic volunteer engagement and program design that does not depend on the contribution of 'super volunteers'.  Of course, if and when your organization has one or more of these wonderful people you will celebrate that enthusiastically (with and about them).   

But be aware – these are the volunteers who can burn out or just decide to leave - which is totally their right, of course - and you may find the hole where they were can seem pretty impossible to fill.   So, celebrate them yes, but avoid total dependence on them as individuals - or the general approach of expecting a lot from a very few. 

During the International Year of Volunteers (2001) at Volunteer Canada, we borrowed from the corporate sector and developed the use of 'Job Design' theory within volunteer programming.  Boiled down to its simplest explanation, this mean more consciously designing a volunteer program that offers a range of volunteer opportunities across a continuum of complexity and time consumption.   

The model challenges those who manage volunteer programs to not use the traditional 'one size fits all' approach to recruitment i.e. "Please come and be a volunteer with us – details on what you'll do and how much time will be involved will be worked out as we go along". 

The Job Design approach suggests that the "jobs" which are created should not all be the same and each one should be clearly outlined in a job description - which also delineates the term and weekly time commitment.  The range of ways to contribute are then varied; there are positions that require little time, or skills, while others need high or very specific skills and a great deal of time.  Or the opposite i.e. low skill/lots of time… high skill but few hours etc. 

This checkerboard approach offers people with less time to give, or who don't have a lot of work experience or specialized knowledge, choice in how they can get involved.  Good job descriptions are also a powerful recruitment tool making very clear what help (and how much) is needed.  And just as with paid positions they are a helpful and necessary management tool. 

Volunteer programs that recruit different kinds of people to fill different kinds of jobs are more robust and better able to withstand the natural ebb and flow of volunteer's participation. Most importantly they will better ensure the achievement of organizational missions and goals. 

A little case example: 

Like most temples, mosques, synagogues and churches, my family church in Ottawa ran a pretty traditional children's learning program – our Sunday School.  We are all familiar with the model; a handful (if you're lucky) of people agree to teach in the program for a number of months – generally over the school year – in a program which usually runs parallel to worship (often in the basement!).  As a result, those volunteers rarely get to attend services.  And over the years they have been harder and harder to recruit.   

I was asked to run the Sunday School at St. Bart's, and I decided to apply the Job Design theory I had been touting across Canada.  I stood up one September morning and said, "We need to recruit Sunday School teachers and we're using a slightly different approach; I'd like to ask you all to consider giving one or two Sundays between now and June…".   

Our program got a tremendous response.  No one wanted to take on the weekly commitment of teaching the kids, but doing one or two mornings?  No problem.  This being Rockliffe in Ottawa, we ended up with a Senator (parliamentary, not hockey!) a judge, a Member of Parliament and a handful of lawyers, doctors and some pretty great teens too.  Our children were exposed to a fascinating group of interesting and different adults, our program flourished, and people who would never have considered teaching in the Sunday School gladly gave the limited amount of time and effort required.

Issue #3: Staffing 

The Challenge 

Based on our experience at MAS, and my own as a 35 years+ Executive Director, there are a myriad of challenges and issues facing charities and not-for-profit organizations when it comes to human resources and staffing.  Addressing them all would be a paper in an of itself and so at this juncture I will only comment on one matter that arose from our Client Survey. 

Many respondents provided feedback on their worry and frustration with not being able to pay adequate enough wages to properly compensate staff, and to address recruitment and retention issues.  The vast majority of community based NFPs have faced this reality and only the very largest, in the best resourced sub-sectors such as health care, tend to have wages that are competitive with other areas of the labour market. 

A Possible Response 

Notwithstanding the ever-present reality facing many charitable organisations in terms of inadequate funding, and not to in any way undermine valiant efforts to increase their funding bases, there is a way, in some instances, to allocate resources to allow for higher compensation rates without having to raise more money.  And it actually relates directly to the previous discussion on volunteer programming. 

The largest component of any NFPs budget is always salaries.  We are a sector of people who "do" things – we are service oriented vs. production.  I have worked in charities where the salary line is 95% of the total annual budget.  In most cases leadership in the organization have a lot of latitude on how they allocate those monies by position (unless there are funder constraints, or the environment is unionized in which case the strategy I am about to recommend would need to be discussed with those parties). 

Without going into a lot of detail (please feel free to contact MAS is you want more guidance though) one of the first steps in the Job Design methodology is an analysis of the full range of functions and tasks within the organization required to achieve the mission – without any consideration as to which work is paid for and which done by volunteers.   

At some point you will determine which element so the work you want or need to pay for (bearing in the mind that many organizations have only volunteer workers).  Normally, paid positions tend to encompass a broader range of tasks than volunteer ones – we would rarely hire someone to do just one thing while we may often engage a volunteer in a single-task job.  

The goal for any well-designed human resource plan is to use mission as the starting point for designing a matrix of jobs - paid and unpaid, full and/or part time etc. However, this is often not how it's actually done.   

Rather, the "real" work is seen to be done by paid people, while volunteer contributions are a nice 'add on'.  (Again, the exception to this being in those groups that have no paid people at all - where obviously everything is done by unpaid individuals).   

I confess that in my own time in planning and management – most often in mid size charities with paid staff cohorts - we rarely considered the volunteer element in terms of delivering on "essential" services.  The assumption was that work would be done – could only be done – by paid people.  (Kind of ironic considering my ten-year stint as the head of Volunteer Canada). 

But the fact is you can get a lot of impact – directly related to core service delivery – from a well-designed volunteer program.  More importantly, effective use of the volunteer element of human resources can give you a lot more creative wiggle room on how to allocate your financial resources for paid staff. 

When I was leading St. Felix Centre, a community-based place offering a range of services for homeless, low income, newcomer and marginalized folks in the downtown west core of Toronto, we had a rule.  Whenever we wanted to expand an existing program or develop a new one, our first question was never 'Can we find the money to hire someone to do this'.  It was 'Lets find the right volunteers to pull this off'.  I was also, in my role there as Executive Director, determined to lift the salaries of the small team of paid staff who worked alongside an amazing 700+ volunteers… 

St. Felix Centre had (still has I believe) a meal program.  When I started in 2008, they were serving around 7,000 meals a year and while there were volunteers involved in serving the meals (cafeteria style) very few were involved in the cooking or client related tasks, which were left to a few (very underpaid) staff i.e. one full time, two part time folks.   

Working with the long time and amazing kitchen manager and cook Iris, we identified a much more diverse set of roles for volunteers from ordering, picking up, storing, preparing food, to engaging with clients of the program, table service (a powerful way to connect with people not often served meals in this way), setting up, cleaning up, and more.  We decided to have only one paid person working in the program and guiding the volunteers.  From that point on Iris's job changed from being the cook, to being the supervisor, teacher and support for the teams of volunteers cooks we recruited.   

Fairly quickly (through attrition) the paid staffing allocation was one salaried position. Within six months we had brought on 300 new volunteers using all the elements of Job Design discussed above – planning, job descriptions etc. as well as a multi faceted shift schedule and a recruitment strategy that asked volunteers to sign on to at least one 3-hour shift a week.  We provided 20,000 meals that first year.  Just as importantly I was able to double the salary of the program manager. 

A final thought on paying for higher salaries: 

My personal philosophy has always been to operate within a sufficiency mindset ie. There is THIS much money available… THIS is our mission… HERE is how we can use those monies toward achieving that mission…  

So, whatever the bottom-line amount of dollars in the budget is, and within a variety of constraints such as Board guidance/approval, funding contracts, existing commitments etc., my goal is always to allocate those monies as creatively as possible, while staying laser focused on the core mission/desired outcome.  I should note that I have applied this approach in organizations ranging in budget size from $50K a year to $7.5M. 

I am not advocating for a replication of my personal approach but am rather adjuring readers of this essay to think outside the proverbial box when it comes to the allocation of funds to human resources.   

Have fewer paid people… pay them more… fill the gaps with volunteers, cleverly designing your volunteer program to be always renewing and never asking too much of any one individua).  

You likely won't stop wanting to bring in more funding as discussed above, and you will need to work with paid staff to change their mindset in terms of working alongside volunteers as peers, but this ultimately simple approach, depending on your unique context, is one you may wish to consider to address the challenge of paying your people more, and more competitively.

Issue #4: Strategic Planning 

The Challenge 

Requests for help with strategic planning is MAS's most common request.  Our volunteer consultants carry out around 150 strategic plan projects every year. From very large, to very small or new organizations, there is a pervasive sense that they must have a strategic plan. 

In much of my experience as an Executive Director, starting in the late eighties, I didn't really encounter strategic planning.  Not until the 2000's certainly.  It just wasn't a common practice and I'm not totally sure where the avid desire to engage in formal strategic planning came from - vs. good operational planning and Board governance planning, both of which have always been an element of NFP work. 

It seems to me that the 2000's brought a trend into charity work that shifted us to more corporate modes of operating.  Others more learned than me in these things could address this better.  But there is no doubt that along with things like outcome measurement, logic models, and accrual accounting, Strategic Plans became a fixture and, in some cases, a significant burden for Boards and senior staff both.   

I have been told many stories of super costly strategic planning processes – up to $50,000 – even in only mid sized organizations.  I have been told of Strategic Plans that get drawn up and then are literally never looked at again.  I have seen firsthand Boards, early in their formation, still struggling with the basics of governance, trying to establish strategic directions long before their organization is ready. 

A possible response 

While I would not advocate eliminating the use of strategic plans – if nothing else the shared exercise that staff and volunteers and Board embark on in the process can have really positive impacts on cohesion and commitment – I certainly think they can be significantly simplified.  

I personally recommend to many organizations that they create a 'one page' strategic plan that articulates a vision, a mission and three to four high level goals for the 'what' the organization seeks to achieve over the next three years.  A secondary annual operational plan will complement this by providing details on 'how'. 

I also have a personal bugaboo about strategic plans which almost always identify strategies for growing… doing "more" etc. (As discussed above in Funding).  Why is it not ok to have a strategic goal for "doing what we do now, really really well for the next few years"?  Do the strategies always have to lay out something new?  Personally, I don't think so. 

I am not alone in my view that the time has come for a serious re-think about strategic planning in the not-for-profit world.  The thoughts below are only a smattering of what others are beginning to think and write.  I'll leave them to further challenge readers on this pervasive and timely topic.  And moving forward I would strongly recommend a discussion at Board tables, and further reading and research, to identify and discuss the future approach of your organization when it comes to strategic planning.   

"There has been a growing acknowledgment that the realities and issues facing the nonprofit community are ever-changing and evolving from year to year, presenting questions about the efficacy of the traditional 3–5-year strategic planning process".  

 The Revolution of Nonprofit Strategic Planning: Fusing Tradition with Innovation, Jaron Bernstein, MBA, Alford Group, August 2024 

 

"BoardSource has also seen a shift away from traditional strategic plans based on three- to five-year time horizons and long planning processes. There has been a move toward strategic frameworks articulating organizational priorities, business plans that combine programmatic and operational goals with financial forecasts, as well as more robust annual plans with clear metrics and timelines".  

Board Source: Resources for Nonprofit Boards; "Nonprofit Strategy and Planning" 2024 

 

"This practice of taking significant time off to determine your strategies and priorities is not only extremely aggravating, but it's also steeped in privilege and is harmful to not only the nonprofits that rely on you for funding, but to the people who rely on the nonprofits."  

Nonprofit AF newsletter, Vu Le, 2023 

 

"When you should not develop a new nonprofit strategic plan: 

When there is a lack of stability at your nonprofit 

When there is a lack of resources (financial, human) 

When the organization is having an identity crisis 

When external factors are dominating the landscape 

In these instances, we advocate for shorter-term planning that focuses on helping nonprofits identify two to three priorities or things that need to be accomplished over the next six months or one year". 

Prosper Strategies, Lindsay Mullen, September 2023 

 

"It's time for some truth-telling. The nonprofit sector has created a culture in which strategic work is seen as a necessary evil, a process to endure, something to suffer through…. When it comes to strategic planning, chief executives often feel sure they know the right answer and already have a sense of what needs to get done in the next few years. Board members will tell you their voices aren't heard or valued. Precious few board members find making plans invigorating or enriching, nor are they excited to promote a new strategy to potential donors".  

Strategic Planning Sucks the Life Out of Nonprofits, Joan Garry (First published in the Chronicle of Philanthropy on Oct 23, 2019)
